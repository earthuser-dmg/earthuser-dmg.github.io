01 / Context

A shared platform.
A fragmented experience.

Making sense of the business before designing the system.

I joined Decathlon as a Product Designer in 2022. My first project was the Business Module System (BMS), a design framework for an internal integration platform. The concept was new to stakeholders, design assets were incomplete, and I was learning a new industry.

The platform connected domain-specific tools rather than operating each business product itself. My scope included modules used in inventory management, demand forecasting and other supply-chain workflows.

Start with the people and the constraints

I explored four questions: the product vision, user needs, available UX support and our team’s mission. Supply Planning Leads faced a steep learning curve, while design support varied: some teams had one designer, others relied on product managers assembling existing components.

02 / Task 1

Build a foundation
teams can reuse.

Consolidate assets before scaling the system.

The problem

Frontend teams were using Ant Design 4.0, sometimes inconsistently. The internal design system was still under construction, and existing design files did not provide a complete starting point.

The decision

I built on the available design system instead of starting from scratch. I compared other systems to understand their structure, technology and design principles.

From scattered assets to a working library

I defined grids, patterns, icons and components that I could reuse in subsequent design tasks. This library also became a foundation for BMS.

04 / Task 2

Design around tasks,
not isolated screens.

Turn recurring business needs into reusable modules.

Daily work crossed internal and external tools, making workflows long and data consistency difficult. As order management, warehouse operations and ERP were integrated into one platform, the interaction patterns also needed to become more consistent.

Combine atomic design with task-driven flows

Many supply-chain tasks repeat. I used atomic design as a structural reference and organized BMS around the steps employees needed to complete, rather than only around individual UI components.

Research, categorize, prioritize

Desk research and a kick-off workshop helped clarify what teams needed. I grouped the content by user behavior, usage frequency and internal priority.

Evolve the content structure

The first structure came from the component library and current design tasks. Later, stakeholder journeys and product reviews helped distinguish business-specific interactions from general services.

Business needs

Interactions tied to particular business processes and product types.

Basic services

Three core asset types

BMS introduced modules, templates and pages. Modules were reusable units; templates and pages provided more customized, ready-to-use solutions.

Make every module understandable

The documentation connected real product use cases with guidelines and examples. For charts, I defined common sizes, layout rules and a color palette for a 1440px canvas. Feedback modals had different frameworks for different scenarios.

Test through actual design requests

I applied high-priority modules to real product work and conducted user testing. Task completion rate and user feedback informed the evaluation.

05 / Collaboration

Make requests
part of the system.

A shared library also needs a way for teams to work together.

Priorities came from real delivery needs

Designers from other product teams asked me to prioritize components they needed for ongoing work. Those requests led to a collaborative model for aligning priorities across teams.

We established an SOP defining the request process between product design teams and the BMS team.

Carry decisions into implementation

I delivered design specifications to frontend engineers as both a reference and a requirements document, and aligned with them on implementation.

06 / Task 3

Build UX practice
through delivery.

Introduce principles while working on everyday product problems.

The starting point

When I joined, the previous designer had left. Design was not consistently involved throughout development, and implementations could diverge from the designs without a clear rationale.

Work through the constraints

For nearly a year, I was the team’s only designer, working across three to four products. Alongside delivery, I shared UX principles and formalized collaboration practices.

I observed collaborating teams progressing from limited UX practice toward more emergent and structured ways of working. This was a qualitative assessment of how we worked together.

07 / Outcomes

What was delivered.
What remains uncertain.

Separate the delivery record from claims of impact.

75% / 10%

At the delivery snapshot, 75% of basic service modules and 10% of business modules were complete. These figures describe library coverage at that stage.

80.07 / 34 responses

The annual UEMS evaluation reported a usability score of 80.07, based on 34 qualified responses from 36 collected.

The measurement limit

There was no pre-UEMS baseline for comparison. The score describes the evaluated experience; it cannot establish how much BMS improved learnability or reduced training costs.

The library, documentation and request process supported more consistent product work. The scale of the efficiency improvement needs additional measurement evidence.

08 / Reflection

From building assets
to sustaining a system.

The next challenge is adoption and ongoing ownership.

BMS was moving from a build phase toward a run phase. The next steps were to extend adoption across products, secure more development resources and keep the library up to date.

What I contributed

  • Researched product scenarios and defined the module structure.
  • Prioritized reusable content and designed module interactions.
  • Tested modules in product work and gathered user feedback.
  • Documented specifications and coordinated engineering handoff.
  • Established a request process and promoted cross-team adoption.
